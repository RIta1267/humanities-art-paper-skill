# Paper Architecture

Use this reference for topic design, title refinement, outlines, opening reports, thesis chapters, and journal manuscripts.

## Research Spine

Diagnose every paper with this chain:

1. Research object: the concrete phenomenon, work, group, practice, archive, text, image, score, institution, policy, region, or historical period.
2. Research problem: the unresolved why/how/what-changed question.
3. Existing explanation: what scholarship, public discourse, or disciplinary habit already says.
4. Tension: what does not fit, remains unexamined, or is overgeneralized.
5. Material basis: fieldwork, interviews, archives, local gazetteers, scores, images, catalogs, performances, policy texts, databases, teaching cases, or other sources.
6. Method: textual analysis, historical analysis, ethnography, iconography, discourse analysis, music morphology, comparative case study, reception study, institutional analysis, or mixed methods.
7. Main claim: the paper's answer, not merely its theme.
8. Contribution: conceptual correction, new material, method transfer, case explanation, historical reconstruction, or practical implication.

## Common Structures

Journal article:

1. Introduction: problem, literature position, materials, method, claim.
2. Context or genealogy: explain the historical/social/artistic background needed for the case.
3. Core analysis: 2-4 sections, each with a claim and evidence.
4. Discussion: theoretical implication, mechanism, comparison, or limits.
5. Conclusion: answer the question and state contribution without exaggeration.

Thesis/dissertation chapter:

1. Chapter question.
2. Relationship to the whole thesis.
3. Materials and method.
4. Section-by-section argument.
5. Interim conclusion that prepares the next chapter.

Opening report:

1. Topic origin and research value.
2. Literature review organized by debate or theme.
3. Research object, questions, and key concepts.
4. Materials, methods, and feasibility.
5. Expected structure and innovation.
6. Schedule and risks.

## Title Rules

Prefer titles that show object plus problem. Avoid titles that only show theme.

Weak: "Research on the Inheritance of X Folk Songs"

Better: "Institutional Mediation and Repertoire Reorganization in the Contemporary Transmission of X Folk Songs"

Use subtitles when the material scope matters:

"From Ritual Soundscape to Classroom Repertoire: A Study of X Folk Songs Based on Y County Field Materials"

## Outline Rules

For every outline, make each section carry a claim, not just a topic label.

Weak:

1. Background of X.
2. Current situation of X.
3. Problems of X.
4. Countermeasures.

Better:

1. Category formation: how X became an object of policy, scholarship, or performance.
2. Material reorganization: how repertoire, image, space, or teaching content changed.
3. Institutional mediation: how schools, troupes, museums, platforms, or local government reshaped practice.
4. Interpretive consequence: what this case changes in our understanding of inheritance, art form, or cultural memory.

## Failure Modes

- Topic too large: reduce by region, period, material type, repertoire, institution, or one controversy.
- No question: convert "study X" into "why/how X changed, was classified, circulated, institutionalized, aestheticized, taught, displayed, or remembered."
- Theory overload: remove concepts that do not change the reading of the material.
- Evidence mismatch: do not use policy discourse to prove performance practice, or interview impressions to prove long historical continuity, unless triangulated.
