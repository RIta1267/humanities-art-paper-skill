# Workflow Gates

Use this reference for staged, end-to-end humanities/social-science/arts paper work. It adapts mature skill patterns into a humanistic writing workflow: route by task, confirm the paper's spine, create a terminology ledger, require evidence mapping, draft after planning, review top-down, and finish with an integrity status.

## Gate 1: Intake

Collect only the information needed for the next move:

- Genre: course paper, journal article, thesis chapter, opening report, project proposal, review response, presentation.
- Field: musicology, art theory, art education, folklore, ICH, cultural studies, theatre/opera, dance, visual art, media, museum studies, or another field.
- Target: journal, degree program, course, funding body, conference, or internal review.
- Materials: draft, notes, fieldwork, interviews, archives, images, scores, policy texts, local gazetteers, tables, bibliography.
- Output needed now: diagnosis, outline, literature review, revision, paragraph draft, abstract, figure/table plan, or response letter.

If the user gives a broad request, do not ask a long checklist. Make reasonable assumptions and identify the 1-3 missing facts that would change the answer.

## Gate 2: One-Sentence Argument

Before outlining or drafting a substantial paper, try to write:

> This paper explains [object/change/tension] through [materials/method], arguing that [claim] within [scope/boundary].

If this sentence is weak, return a diagnosis instead of a full draft:

- Object is too broad.
- Change/tension is unclear.
- Materials do not support the claim.
- Method is only a label.
- Contribution is a slogan rather than an academic move.

Offer 2-4 alternative research questions with required evidence.

## Gate 3: Terminology Ledger

Create a compact ledger when the task involves multiple concepts, translations, names, places, or categories.

Fields:

- Term.
- Canonical Chinese form.
- Optional English translation.
- Scope in this paper.
- Avoided synonym or ambiguity.
- Source basis if provided.

Use the ledger to prevent concept drift. Examples: "活态传承", "地方性知识", "曲牌", "声腔", "展演", "非遗进校园", "ritual soundscape", "vernacular modernity".

## Gate 4: Evidence Map

Map major claims before drafting:

| Claim | Evidence now available | Evidence still needed | Claim strength |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| [claim] | [material] | [gap] | strong / plausible / speculative |

Rules:

- Strong: supported by direct material and contextual corroboration.
- Plausible: supported by partial material but needs framing.
- Speculative: useful as a question, not yet a conclusion.

Do not upgrade a speculative claim through confident language.

## Gate 5: Section Blueprint

Before full prose, make a section blueprint:

| Section | Section claim | Materials used | Paragraph jobs | Risk |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |

Paragraph jobs may include:

- Context.
- Problem.
- Literature position.
- Concept definition.
- Material/method.
- Close analysis.
- Historical reconstruction.
- Comparison.
- Mechanism.
- Implication.
- Limitation.

Each paragraph should have one main job. Split paragraphs that carry two unrelated jobs.

Run a section-parity check before drafting:

- Put every first-level heading into one role: introduction, literature review, materials/method, analysis, discussion, conclusion, appendix, or author note.
- Same-level analysis sections must answer the same kind of question. Examples: three functions, three periods, three regions, three material types, or three mechanisms.
- Do not flatten different roles into one sequence. A scope/boundary section such as "From Jiangsu to the Yangtze River Delta" is usually a discussion section, not a peer of the core analysis sections.
- If sections mix levels, restructure before drafting.

Bad pattern:

1. Introduction.
2. Materials and method.
3. Ecological background.
4. Emotional metaphor.
5. Counting games.
6. From one province to a region.
7. Conclusion.

Better pattern:

1. Introduction.
2. Materials and method.
3. Imagery spectrum and ecological basis.
4. Expression mechanisms: labor, emotion, play.
5. Discussion: from one province to a regional comparison.
6. Conclusion.

Alternative parallel analysis pattern:

1. Introduction.
2. Materials and method.
3. Ecological anchoring of biological imagery.
4. Affective mediation of biological imagery.
5. Performative and game functions of biological imagery.
6. Discussion: scope, limits, and regional extension.
7. Conclusion.

## Gate 6: Visual Evidence Plan

Before drafting a substantial paper, decide whether the paper needs tables, figures, captions, or legends.

Use visuals when they do at least one of these jobs:

- Make the material scope transparent.
- Show coding or classification criteria.
- Compress comparison across regions, periods, genres, actors, works, or image categories.
- Clarify a mechanism, sequence, genealogy, spatial relation, or analytical hierarchy.
- Let the reader verify how examples support a section claim.

Do not add a visual when it only repeats prose, decorates the article, or creates false quantification from weak material.

Required visual plan:

| Figure/table | Section placement | Claim supported | Material basis | Caption/legend need | Risk |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| [title] | [section] | [claim] | [source/data] | [categories/symbols/source note] | [permission/evidence/overclaim] |

Caption and legend checks:

- Every table or figure must be mentioned in the prose before or immediately after it appears.
- Captions should name the source basis, not only say "author self-made."
- Legends must explain colors, symbols, category names, abbreviations, and coding marks.
- Tables based on OCR, excerpts, field notes, or incomplete catalog data must mark the limitation.
- Figures using images, maps, scores, archives, or museum materials need permission and reproduction checks.

For detailed visual design patterns, read `figures-tables.md`.

## Gate 7: Draft Or Revise

For drafting:

1. Draft from section claims and evidence map, not from generic background.
2. Keep citations as placeholders only when the user has not provided verified references.
3. State missing evidence as author checks.

For revising:

1. Diagnose argument and structure first.
2. Then revise paragraphs.
3. Preserve the user's strongest concepts and disciplinary voice.
4. Do not erase useful historical, material, or interpretive nuance for generic smoothness.

## Gate 8: Review Gate

Before the integrity audit, use layered review when the user asks for critique, defense preparation, external-review response, or submission readiness.

Four-layer critique:

1. Foundation: research question, object, claim, contribution, and evidence fit.
2. Structure: section order, section claims, transitions, repetition, and missing steps.
3. Paragraph function: each paragraph's job, claim, evidence, and relation to the section.
4. Sentence style: clarity, rhythm, terminology, register, and unnecessary abstraction.

Reviewer simulation:

- Concept attack: which key terms are vague, overloaded, or imported without necessity?
- Evidence attack: which claims outrun the material?
- Literature attack: which adjacent scholarship could challenge the claimed gap?
- Method attack: where are method and material only named rather than used?
- Scope attack: where do title, abstract, or conclusion promise too much?

For review outputs, lead with the highest-risk issues, then provide revision moves. Do not start with copyediting.

## Gate 9: Integrity Audit

Before final delivery, check:

- Fabrication: citations, fieldwork facts, archives, dates, statistics, page numbers, interviews, images, scores.
- Evidence mismatch: claim stronger than material.
- Scope creep: title, abstract, and conclusion promise more than the paper proves.
- Concept drift: key terms used inconsistently.
- Literature gap inflation: "no research exists" without proof.
- Theory decoration: concepts named but not used analytically.
- Translation flattening: English terms lose local disciplinary meaning.
- Voice loss: revision has erased the author's conceptual emphasis or disciplinary texture.

Mark unresolved issues clearly.

## Gate 10: Delivery Status

End substantial outputs with one of:

- `ready`: the output can be used as-is for the requested purpose.
- `needs author check`: the structure is usable but facts, citations, or material details require confirmation.
- `blocked`: the task cannot proceed responsibly without specific missing information.

For `needs author check` and `blocked`, list the exact checks or missing items.

## Quick Mode

If the user asks for a fast sketch, produce a lightweight version:

1. One-sentence argument attempt.
2. 3-5 part outline.
3. Evidence gap list.
4. Next writing move.

Still do not invent sources or materials.
