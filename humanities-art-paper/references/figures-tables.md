# Figures And Tables

Use this reference for humanities and arts research diagrams, maps, timelines, tables, legends, and figure/table captions.

The default standard is simple: a table or figure must help the reader see the evidence chain. If it only makes the paper look more "academic," remove it.

## Figure Types

Choose figures that clarify argument, not decorate the paper:

- Timeline: policy stages, repertoire transformation, institution history, artist career, publication/performance chronology.
- Transmission genealogy: master-apprentice, troupe, school, family, regional lineage, institutional inheritance.
- Fieldwork map: research sites, migration/communication routes, performance circuits, festival locations.
- Repertoire table: title, genre, source, performer, occasion, text theme, mode/tune, collection date.
- Work analysis table: structure, section, motif, rhythm, image element, gesture, scene, material, interpretation.
- Actor-institution network: artists, associations, schools, troupes, museums, platforms, government agencies.
- Process diagram: ritual sequence, teaching process, heritage nomination process, exhibition production workflow.
- Comparison matrix: before/after, region A/B, stage/ritual/classroom/platform versions.
- Imagery taxonomy: animals/plants/material objects/symbolic clusters and their analytical relations.
- Function matrix: image type by expressive function, performance scene, labor context, ritual function, affective role, or narrative job.
- Evidence flow diagram: from source collection to cleaning, coding, close reading, comparison, and argument.

## Figure Question Gate

Before designing a figure, answer:

1. What analytical question does the figure answer?
2. What material or data supports it?
3. What would the reader misunderstand without this figure?
4. Is the figure a diagram, table, map, timeline, network, or close-analysis plate?

If the figure only repeats prose or decorates the page, recommend no figure.

## Visual Evidence Gate

Before drafting, create a compact plan:

| Figure/table | Paper section | Question answered | Evidence basis | Caption/legend need |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| [title] | [section] | [question] | [materials] | [source/categories/symbols] |

Decide against a table or figure when:

- The categories are not yet defined.
- The material is too thin to compare.
- The visual would imply quantitative certainty that the paper does not have.
- The same point is clearer in one paragraph.

## Table Types For Humanities Papers

Use tables for evidence transparency, coding, comparison, and section control.

Source inventory table:

| Item | Example fields |
| --- | --- |
| Song/work/source | title, region, genre, collector/editor, page or archive location, text status |
| Use in paper | quoted, counted, background only, excluded |
| Verification | checked against original, OCR only, uncertain, needs author check |

Imagery or motif coding table:

| Item | Example fields |
| --- | --- |
| Object coded | image/motif/term, category, exact excerpt, genre, region |
| Interpretation | scene, function, affect, performance role, evidence strength |
| Limits | ambiguous use, title-only occurrence, duplicate, uncertain OCR |

Regional or historical comparison table:

| Item | Example fields |
| --- | --- |
| Comparison unit | region, period, troupe, repertoire type, ritual scene, institution |
| Shared feature | common image, form, practice, terminology, performance condition |
| Difference | local ecology, dialect, genre, institution, audience, medium |

Section-evidence map:

| Section | Section claim | Main examples | Table/figure used | Missing check |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| [section] | [claim] | [materials] | [visual] | [gap] |

This table is useful during drafting even if it is not included in the final paper.

## Figure Types For Folk Song, Art, And ICH Papers

For folk song and folklore papers:

- Imagery spectrum diagram: show categories such as aquatic life, birds, crops, flowers, trees, insects, domestic animals, and mythical/ritual animals.
- Imagery-function matrix: connect object types to labor rhythm, courtship, counting games, ritual address, satire, knowledge transmission, or local landscape.
- Corpus processing flow: show source volume, extraction, cleaning, manual verification, coding, and close-reading sample.
- Regional comparison schema: show Jiangsu/Zhejiang/Shanghai/Anhui as comparison units while marking missing materials.
- Song-type distribution table: compare haozi, shange, xiaodiao, ritual song, children's song, and custom song.

For visual art, design, and museum studies:

- Image plate with numbered callouts.
- Exhibition-route diagram.
- Object metadata table.
- Material/technique comparison table.
- Actor-institution network.

For musicology and performance studies:

- Lyrics-melody alignment table.
- Performance-scene table.
- Rhythm/form comparison table.
- Repertoire transmission genealogy.

## Caption Logic

A caption should state:

1. What the figure shows.
2. Source or data basis.
3. Abbreviation or classification explanation.
4. Why it matters if the figure is analytically dense.

Do not use "author self-made" as the only source note if the figure depends on fieldwork, archives, images, or databases. State the underlying material.

Captions should behave like mini-claims. A weak caption only names the object; a strong caption tells the reader what to notice and how the evidence was assembled.

Weak:

> Figure 1. Biological imagery in folk songs. Source: author self-made.

Better:

> Figure 1. Biological imagery categories identified in the Jiangsu volume, grouped by ecological scene and expressive function. Source basis: extracted song titles, lyrics excerpts, and genre notes from the named corpus; OCR results require manual verification.

For tables, use a note when categories are interpretive:

> Note: "Affective metaphor" and "labor regulation" are analytical categories assigned by the author after checking the lyric context; title-only occurrences are marked separately.

## Legend Rules

Use a legend when the reader must decode visual signs.

A legend should explain:

- Colors.
- Shapes.
- Line types.
- Region or period abbreviations.
- Category names.
- Coding marks such as checked, uncertain, excluded, inferred, or title-only.

Avoid legends that introduce more categories than the paper actually analyzes. If a legend has ten colors but the section discusses three relations, simplify the figure.

## In-Text Reference Rules

Every visual needs a prose job.

Before the table or figure, prepare the reader:

> Table 2 shows why the high frequency of "flower" cannot be read as a single symbolic category.

After the table or figure, interpret it:

> The table suggests that plant images move between three uses: local ecology, affective metaphor, and performance/game structure.

Do not let the caption carry the whole argument.

## Design Rules

- Keep Chinese labels short and legible.
- Use restrained color and sufficient contrast.
- Avoid complex 3D effects, decorative gradients, and unreadable dense networks.
- For journal submission, prefer editable vector formats when possible.
- For maps, state whether geography is schematic or georeferenced.
- For music/image analysis, align labels with the exact passage, frame, section, or visual element being discussed.

## Table Rules

- Put interpretive categories in columns only if the criteria are explicit.
- Avoid tables that repeat prose without comparison.
- Use notes for source limits, missing values, translation, or classification decisions.
- Separate raw occurrence, manually verified occurrence, and interpreted function.
- Do not rank concepts by frequency unless the corpus boundary and counting method are clear.
- In humanities papers, a small table of carefully verified examples is often stronger than a large table of unstable counts.

## Recommended Output

When asked to design a figure/table, provide:

- Figure/table title.
- Purpose in the argument.
- Required materials or data fields.
- Draft structure.
- Caption draft.
- Legend draft when symbols, colors, line types, or abbreviations are used.
- In-text placement and the sentence that introduces the visual.
- Source note.
- Warnings about evidence or permissions.

## Figure QA

Before finalizing a figure plan, check:

- Argument fit: the figure supports a section claim.
- Source traceability: every element can be traced to material.
- Label clarity: terms match the paper's terminology ledger.
- Permission risk: images, scores, maps, and archival materials may need rights checks.
- Reproducibility: the author could rebuild the figure from the listed materials.
